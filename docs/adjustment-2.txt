Cosine and sine work as a team

What is a sound?
A sound is a fast vibration moving through the air. To vibrate is to move back and forth quickly. A speaker cone moves out and in, hundreds of times a second. Each push shoves the air in front of it, and that push travels across the room to your ear.

Watch the air between the speaker and the ear. Where the dots bunch together, the air is squeezed tight. That squeeze is a compression. Where they spread apart, the air is thin. That gap is a rarefaction. The pattern of squeeze and spread travels left to right, reaches your eardrum, and your brain calls the whole thing sound.

Press play to send a wave from the speaker to the ear. Drag the pitch slider and the squeezes pack closer together. Drag the loudness slider and each push grows bigger.

That line is called a waveform. Left to right is time. Up and down is the push of the air, with the compressions above the middle and the rarefactions below. Every sound you have ever heard is a version of this wave.

Pitch is how fast it vibrates
Vibrate faster and the sound is higher. Vibrate slower and it is lower. How fast it vibrates is the frequency, measured in hertz (Hz), which means vibrations per second. Each vibration is one of those compressions reaching your ear, so more of them per second reads as a higher note.

One rule is worth knowing. Double the frequency and you rise one octave, the same note higher. The note A is 440 Hz. Double it to 880 Hz and it is A again, higher up. Pitch is speed.

Loudness is how big the wave is
Loudness is not pitch. Loudness is how big the wave is, not how fast. A bigger wave moves more air, so it sounds louder. The size of the wave is its amplitude. A larger amplitude is a harder push of air against your eardrum.

The sonic prism
Have you seen white light pass through a prism? The light spreads into colors. The white light was not one color; it contained many wavelengths at once.

A Fourier Transform is like a prism for sound. A complex sound goes in, and a frequency recipe comes out.

A speaker's sound wave entering a glass prism and fanning out the other side into separate wavy lines, with the Fourier transform formula engraved on the prism.
One complex wave enters. Its frequency ingredients fan out.
Choose a sound and send it through the prism. Each band is a different frequency. Taller, brighter bands are stronger components in the sound.

The transform does not physically pull the sound apart. It calculates which frequencies are present and how strong each one is. The prism is our visual metaphor.

Give the same thorough treatment to the Part 1 lesson as well

Part 1 is doing the hardest job in the whole sequence: it has to make learners believe that sound has ingredients before Part 2 asks them to trust the math. The current lesson is strong, but it is trying to teach a lot at once: waveform, spectrum, pure tone, harmonics, additive synthesis, formula reading, square waves, bass/mids/treble, and voice analysis.

The core fix is to make Part 1 feel less like “many audio concepts” and more like one clear transformation:

A sound moves → we draw it as a waveform → we read it as a spectrum → we split it into ingredients → we build it back from ingredients.

That through-line should be visible on every page.

Part 1 audit
What is working

The lesson has a strong conceptual arc. It starts with a real-world question, gives learners two visual representations, introduces pure tones, uses the sonic prism metaphor, then lets learners build sound from harmonics. That is the right sequence.

The interactions are also much richer now. You have prediction, tapping, matching, dragging, sliders, live spectrum changes, microphone use, and synthesis. That is exactly the direction for beginner-friendly music tech.

The strongest pages conceptually are:

The sonic prism
Build a sound from pure tones
Same sound, two views
See your own voice
Square wave from sines

Those are the pages that make the lesson feel special.

What needs improvement

The biggest issue is density. Several screenshots show the browser zoomed out because the page is vertically packed. That tells us the interaction is too tall or too many learning moves are living on one screen.

Part 1 also introduces some ideas before learners have enough language for them. For example, “same note, similar loudness” is a great hook, but some learners may not yet know pitch, loudness, timbre, frequency, harmonics, or amplitude. You can still use those words, but the lesson should keep returning to plain-language anchors:

Pitch = which note.
Loudness = how strong.
Timbre = what kind of sound.
Spectrum = ingredient list.
Harmonics = extra tones stacked above the main one.

The formula page is useful, but it needs to be clearly framed as optional or exploratory. It belongs in Part 1 only if the learner has already built tones with sliders. Otherwise, it may feel like math appeared too early.

The square wave page is excellent, but it may be doing too much for Part 1. It can stay, but it should be framed as a “surprise proof” rather than another major formula lesson.

Why can the same note sound completely different?

A flute and a violin can play the same note at the same loudness, and you can still tell them apart. A song can sound thin on tiny earbuds and huge on good headphones. A voice can sound warm, bright, nasal, smooth, sharp, or full.

That is because sound has more inside it than pitch and volume.

A sound has a frequency recipe.

Some ingredients are low.
Some are high.
Some are strong.
Some are quiet.

In this lesson series, you will learn to see and shape that recipe.

The main tool is the Fourier Transform:
FOOR-ee-ay Transform

It answers one powerful question:

Which frequencies are inside this sound, and how strong is each one?

Sound Is a Moving Shape

Revised copy

Sound is vibration.

When something vibrates, it pushes and pulls the air around it.

A microphone turns that movement into numbers.

A speaker turns those numbers back into movement.

So the audio chain looks like this:

moving air → numbers → moving air again

When we draw those changing numbers over time, we get a waveform.

A waveform is not the sound itself. It is a picture of how the sound changes.

Required component patterns:

- Supports route/hash navigation.
- Shows previous/next controls.
- Shows progress dots.

- Larger card for interactive visuals.
- Clear internal title.

- Controls area.
- Live feedback line.
- Optional challenge area.

3. TakeawayCard
- Short final summary for a page.
- Visually distinct from body copy.
- Should not be overly decorative.

4. ChallengeCard
- For formative checks.
- Includes prompt, interaction, feedback, and optional skill badge.

5. AudioButton
- Handles browser audio unlock safely.
- Has clear labels such as Play tone, Play target, Play yours, Play flute, Play violin.
- Avoid generic "Play" when multiple sounds are present.

6. FrequencySlider
- Label, current value, left/right anchors.
- Example anchors:
  - lower pitch / higher pitch
  - quieter / louder
  - low frequency / high frequency
  - instant start / slow start
  - short fade / long fade

7. WaveformDisplay
- Must show Time → on x-axis.
- Must show pressure or loudness on y-axis.
- Supports active sample dots, ghost waveform, and current waveform.

8. SpectrumDisplay
- Must show Frequency, low to high → on x-axis.
- Must show Strength on y-axis.
- Supports bass/mids/treble labels.
- Supports highlighted bars and target bars.

9. FormulaToken
- Used for formulas like y(t) = A sin(2πft + φ) and X[k] = Σ x[n] e^(-i2πkn/N).
- Each token should be clickable or focusable.
- Each token should connect to a plain-English explanation.

10. LiveFeedbackLine
- One sentence that updates as learners interact.
- Example:
  "Higher pitch: the waveform repeats faster and the spectrum bar moves right."

11. SkillBadge or SuccessState
- Used after challenges.
- Example:
  "Skill unlocked: You can identify the stronger frequency."

- Use strong hover/focus states for interactive items.

PAGE 2: What Is a Sound?

Purpose:
Show that sound is moving air, then connect moving air to a waveform.

Rewrite for clarity:

A sound is a vibration moving through the air.

To vibrate means to move back and forth quickly.

A speaker cone moves out and in many times per second. Each push squeezes the air in front of it. That squeeze travels across the room to your ear.

Where the dots bunch together, the air is squeezed. That is compression.

Where the dots spread apart, the air is thinner. That is rarefaction.

The pattern of compression and rarefaction travels from the speaker to your ear. Your eardrum moves with it, and your brain understands that movement as sound.

Interaction:
- Speaker on left.
- Ear on right.
- Dots between them.
- Press Play to send a wave from speaker to ear.
- Pitch slider controls how close together the compression bands are.
- Loudness slider controls how strong each push is.

Second visual:
After the air animation plays, show the waveform below.

Transition copy:
The moving air is the real sound. When we draw that push and pull as a line over time, we get a waveform.

A waveform is a picture of the vibration.

Graph labels:
- Time →
- pressure / loudness

Formative task:
Tap the compression.
Learner taps where dots bunch together.

Feedback:
Yes. Where the dots bunch together, the air is compressed.

Takeaway:
Sound is vibration moving through air. A waveform is that vibration drawn over time.

PAGE 3: Pitch Is How Fast It Vibrates

Purpose:
Make frequency physically visible.

Rewrite:

Pitch is how high or low a sound seems.

A sound with faster vibration has a higher pitch.
A sound with slower vibration has a lower pitch.

The speed of vibration is called frequency.

Frequency is measured in hertz, or Hz.
One hertz means one vibration per second.

So a 330 Hz sound vibrates 330 times per second.

Interaction:
- Play tone.
- Frequency slider.
- Waveform visibly tightens as frequency increases.
- Spectrum or simple note label may update if available.
- Buttons: Octave down, Octave up.

Live feedback examples:
- 330 Hz, near E4.
- Higher pitch: the waveform repeats faster.
- Lower pitch: the waveform repeats slower.

Formative task:
Make the wave tighter.
Learner must move slider higher.

Then:
Make the wave wider.
Learner must move slider lower.

Takeaway:
Pitch is speed. Higher pitch means faster vibration. Lower pitch means slower vibration.

PAGE 4: Loudness Is How Big the Wave Is

Purpose:
Separate loudness from pitch.

Rewrite:

Loudness is not pitch.

Loudness is how strong the sound feels.

On a waveform, loudness is connected to the size of the wave.

A bigger wave pushes more air, so it sounds louder.
The size of the wave is called amplitude.

A larger amplitude means a stronger push against your eardrum.

Interaction:
- Play tone.
- Loudness slider.
- Pitch remains fixed.
- Show ghost waveform behind active waveform.
- Active waveform gets taller or shorter, but spacing stays the same.

Live feedback:
Same pitch, louder sound. The wave is taller, not faster.

Formative task:
Make it louder without changing pitch.

Optional A/B:
Play quiet version.
Play loud version.
Ask:
What changed?
Correct answer:
Loudness changed.

Takeaway:
Pitch is how fast the wave vibrates. Loudness is how big the wave is.

Wave shape is one major part of timbre.

PAGE 6: How a Sound Begins and Ends

Purpose:
Teach envelope as loudness over time.

Rewrite:

A sound is not only a pitch, loudness, and wave shape.

It also has a beginning and an ending.

Pluck a guitar string and the sound jumps in, then fades.

Hold an organ key and the sound swells or sustains.

That shape over time is called the envelope.

An envelope is the shape of a sound’s loudness over time. It describes how the sound starts, changes, and fades.

Subtitle:
Music technology · signals · beginner friendly · about 15 minutes

Why can the same note sound completely different?

PAGE 3: Sound Is a Moving Shape

Purpose:
Connect sound motion, numbers, and waveform.

Copy:
Sound is vibration.

When something vibrates, it pushes and pulls the air around it.

A microphone turns that movement into numbers.

A speaker turns those numbers back into movement.

So the audio chain looks like this:
moving air → numbers → moving air again

When we draw those changing numbers over time, we get a waveform.

A waveform is not the sound itself. It is a picture of how the sound changes.

Interaction:

Numbers: one value per moment
Sound out: moving air again

Then a waveform drawing lab:
Buttons:

Waveform labels:

Push / higher pressure
Pull / lower pressure
Center line

Quick check:

Tap beginning, middle, and end of the wave.

Takeaway:
A waveform shows a sound changing over time. It answers: what is the sound doing moment by moment?

PAGE 9: Build a Sound from Pure Tones

Purpose:
Teach additive synthesis by reversing the analysis process clearly.

Use the updated clear language.

Copy:
The sonic prism showed how to analyze a sound: start with one finished sound and reveal the pure tones inside it.

On this page, you do the opposite.

Start with pure tones.

Stack them together.

Hear them become one new sound.

That is additive synthesis.

Additive synthesis means building a complex sound by adding simple sine waves together.

Start with H1, the fundamental.

Then raise H2, H3, H4, and the rest.

As you add harmonics:
- the sound changes
- the spectrum gains bars
- the waveform becomes more detailed

Concept map:
Analyze:
finished sound → spectrum ingredients

Synthesize:
pure tones → finished sound

Key terms:
Fundamental:
The first and lowest bar. It sets the main pitch.

Harmonics:
Higher tones above the fundamental: 2x, 3x, 4x, and so on.

Amplitude:
The height of a bar. It shows how strong that harmonic is.

Timbre:
The whole pattern of harmonic strengths. This pattern gives the sound its character.

PAGE 10: One Pure Tone, Written as a Formula

Purpose:
Translate sliders into a formula without making it intimidating.

Copy:
Every pure tone you added follows the same pattern.

You can control:
- how tall the wave is
- how fast it repeats
- where it starts

Here is one pure tone written as a formula:

y(t) = A · sin(2πft + φ)

This formula is not here to scare you.

It is just a compact way to describe one smooth tone.

Formula tokens:
y(t): the sound value at this moment in time
A: amplitude, how tall the wave is
sin: the smooth repeating wave shape
2π: one full trip around a cycle
f: frequency, how fast the wave repeats
t: time
φ: phase, where the wave starts

PAGE 12: Exploring Bass, Mids, and Treble

Purpose:
Teach frequency regions for Part 3 EQ and headphone work.

Copy:
A spectrum runs from low frequencies on the left to high frequencies on the right.

We often split that range into three regions:

Bass is the low end.
Mids are the middle.
Treble is the high end.

You do not need to know these already. This screen is for building that map.

Approximate ranges:
Bass: about 20 to 250 Hz. Kick weight, bass guitar, 808s, low synths.
Mids: about 250 Hz to 4 kHz. Voice, body, guitar, piano, melodies.
Treble: about 4 kHz to 20 kHz. Cymbals, air, brightness, edge.

Note:
These ranges are approximate. Real sounds often spread across more than one region.

PAGE 1: How Does a Computer Find the Recipe?

Copy:
You can already look at a spectrum and understand what it means.

But how does a computer create that spectrum?

A computer does not hear sound like a person. It does not know flute, violin, bright, smooth, or buzzy.

It only sees numbers.

A digital sound is a list of quick measurements called samples.

In this part, you will see how those samples become a frequency recipe.

The whole method is simpler than the formula looks:

1. Choose a frequency.
2. Make a test wave at that frequency.
3. Multiply the sound by the test wave, sample by sample.
4. Add the products.
5. See whether the total is big or small.

A big total means:
That frequency is strongly present.

A small total means:
That frequency is weak or mostly missing.

PAGE 5: Multiply, Then Add

Copy:
Now we run the actual frequency test.

For one test frequency:

2. Take the matching sample from the test wave.
3. Multiply them.
4. Drop the product into a running total.

At the end, the total becomes a match score.

A large match score means:
This frequency lines up strongly with the sound.

A small match score means:
This frequency is weak or mostly missing.

PAGE 7: Phase Can Hide a Match

Copy:
There is one problem.

Two waves can have the same frequency but start in different places.

That starting position is called phase.

Frequency tells us how close together the cycles are.

Phase tells us where the wave starts.

If the test wave starts in the wrong place, a single test can miss a frequency that is actually there.

The frequency is right.

The timing is shifted.

PAGE 8: Cosine and Sine Work as a Team

Copy:
To avoid missing a shifted wave, the computer uses two test waves:

cosine and sine.

They test the same frequency from two different starting positions.

Think of them like two spotlights aimed at the same frequency from different angles.

One spotlight may catch what the other misses.

Run the test with both waves.

Call the cosine total C.

Call the sine total S.

Then combine them:

amount = √(C² + S²)

That amount is the strength of the frequency.

Interaction:

Takeaway:
Cosine and sine together measure a frequency’s strength no matter where the wave starts.

PAGE 9: The Formula Is a Shortcut

Copy:
You have already done the transform by feel.

You made a test wave.

You multiplied.

You added.

You checked the total.

Now here is the same method written as the Discrete Fourier Transform:

X[k] = Σ x[n] · e^(-i2πkn/N)

It looks intense, but it is not a new idea.

It means:

For this frequency, multiply every sound sample by the test wave, then add the products.

Plain English:
answer for one frequency = add up sound sample × test wave

Formula tokens:
X[k]: answer for frequency bin k
Σ: add up the products
x[n]: sound sample at position n
n: current sample index
N: total number of samples
k: frequency being tested
e^(-i2πkn/N): the test wave, with cosine and sine packed together

Task:
Build the formula from blocks:
For frequency k → take each sound sample x[n] → multiply by the test wave → add all products → get X[k]

Takeaway:
The formula is the compact version of the frequency test you already understand.

PAGE 10: Pipes and Redirects Move Text Around

Purpose:

Copy:
Many command-line tools work with text.

Example:

Plain English:

A redirect saves output into a file.

Example:
echo "hello" > message.txt

Plain English:

PAGE 11: Permissions and the Shebang

Purpose:
Teach why scripts sometimes do not run.

Copy:
A script is a file.

Before you run a script directly, the computer may need two clues.

The first clue is the shebang.

The shebang is the first line of many scripts:

#!/usr/bin/env bash

It tells the computer:
Run this file with Bash.

The second clue is permission.

A script may need execute permission before you can run it like a program.

Command:
chmod +x script.sh

Plain English:
Allow this script file to run.

Then:
./script.sh

Plain English:
Run the script in the current folder.

Interaction:
Script launch checklist.

Checklist:
Has shebang?
Has execute permission?
Run command uses ./ ?

Learner fixes:
Add shebang
Run chmod +x
Use ./script.sh

Formative task:
The script says Permission denied. What likely needs to change?

Correct:
Give the script execute permission.

Takeaway:
The shebang tells the system which shell to use. Execute permission lets the script run like a program.
